    Polling and scaling¶

    ← Documentation index

    How the poller works, how to tell whether it is keeping up, and what to change when it is not.

    The model¶

    Every device is polled on three cadences, dispatched through Redis-backed queues:

    Poll Default Collects
    Fast 30 s Interface traffic, resources, clients, status
    Logs 60 s Device log events
    Slow 5 min Interfaces, VLANs, system info, wireless, STP

    The intervals are set in Settings → Polling (the defaults above), and a site or a single device can override them, along with MAC scan, config snapshots and whether backups include passwords and keys (#261). The most specific wins: a device's own setting, then its site's, then the global one. A site behind a slow or metered link can be polled gently without slowing the rest.

    • A site: Sites → the site's edit button → Polling and backups. This is the usual place: devices behind the same slow link are normally in the same site.
    • A device: for the odd one out (a unit on an LTE backup link, say), Edit Device → Advanced: polling and backups, which shows each value in force and where it comes from. The same settings are on the device's Overview tab, in a Polling and backups card listing each value and whether it comes from this device, its site or the global setting. Edit there works like Physical Details: tick set for this device on the ones to change, then Save or Cancel.

    Overrides take a fleet admin or that site's admin. Before 0.51.0 the global intervals in Settings were saved but never used: polls ran every 30 seconds, 5 minutes and 1 minute whatever they said.

    Anything that depends on the cadence follows each device's own: a device counts as stale after three missed fast polls (at least 3 minutes), topology links are dropped after four missed slow polls (at least 20 minutes), and headroom adds up each device's own fast-poll rate.

    Enqueue rate is not connect rate. Workers process jobs with limited concurrency, so what matters is whether they drain faster than the schedule fills.

    Where a poll's time goes¶

    Settings → Polling → Where a fast poll's time goes splits a fast poll into three parts (#301):

    • Connecting: the TCP handshake, the TLS handshake (an RSA signature on the router's CPU) and the login. A few round trips, so on a slow link this is often most of the poll.
    • Waiting on the device: the round trips for the reads, and the router answering them.
    • Database and processing: everything on this server.

    It shows the fleet's averages, and below them the slowest fast polls, each device with its own split and a bar, so a slow site can be pinned on its link (connecting and waiting both long), its router (waiting long on a fast link) or this server (the grey part). Hover a failed poll for its error.

    Sessions kept open between polls¶

    Measured on the reference fleet, connecting took 150 to 300 ms per poll on a LAN, against 3 to 25 ms for the commands, and over a WAN each of its round trips adds the link's latency. A device pays it on every fast, log and slow poll.

    Settings → Polling → Keep sessions open between polls keeps an API session open per device and reuses it. It's off by default: long-lived sessions to every device are a change in how the manager behaves on your network, so it's for you to switch on. When it's on:

    • a device has up to three sessions, one each for fast, log and slow polls (they run on separate queues and often at the same moment);
    • a session is reused only while it's healthy and the device's address, port, login and TLS setting are unchanged; any error or timeout closes it rather than handing it on, and a poll that finds its session busy connects on its own;
    • the serial number is re-checked every ten minutes on a reused session;
    • sessions idle for eleven minutes are closed, and TCP keepalive holds them open through NAT and firewalls between polls;
    • switching it off closes the open sessions at each device's next poll.

    On the reference fleet it took fast polls from about 800 ms to 100 to 400 ms; what remains is the collecting itself.

    Slow polls no longer wait for the manager's own ARP scan (it fills in addresses for neighbours that advertise none). It used to expire on the slow poll's own schedule, so every device's slow poll waited up to 12 seconds for a fresh scan; where the scan can't run (Docker Desktop, or no NET_RAW) that was all 12. Polls now use the last finished scan while the next runs in the background. On the reference fleet a slow poll went from 12.5 s to well under a second.

    Reads sent together¶

    A fast poll makes about ten reads (interfaces and their counters, resources, ARP, DHCP leases, the bridge host table, wireless registrations, PoE). They used to go one after another, each waiting for the last to answer: ten round trips. Now they're sent together, each with its own tag, and the answers are sorted back as they arrive (#301). Over a WAN that's one round trip instead of ten, roughly 450 ms saved per poll on a 50 ms link. On a LAN, where the router's own work dominates, the reference devices gained 10 to 25%.

    Only reads are sent this way, and only within one poll; a read that fails (a menu the device doesn't have) fails on its own, as it did before, and any reply that doesn't fit drops the connection. Settings → Polling → Send reads together turns it off.

    Pages that show something from every device¶

    Some pages show what only the device can tell (DHCP servers, traffic-flow settings, the dashboard's security scores). They used to contact every device on every load. Now they show what was last read from each device, with when, and refresh entries older than 10 to 15 minutes in the background, eight devices at a time. A small fleet barely notices: the page waits a moment for those refreshes. A large one gets the last known view at once, and it fills in as the page re-checks every few seconds while refreshes run. Refresh reads every device again (still eight at a time), and a change made on the page drops that device's cached entry so it shows straight away (#302).

    Bulk actions that contact every device ("Check all for updates", "Sync all", deploying an SSID to several access points) also run eight devices at a time.

    Pages on a large fleet¶

    • Choosing a device. Every drop-down that lists devices (Events, Backups, Clients, the Network Services pages, the device Tools tab, Copy VLANs, Guest WiFi and others) can be typed into: every word typed must appear in the device's name, address, site, model or identity, so "amsterdam crs" narrows straight to the switches at that site. Arrow keys and Enter choose, Escape closes, and typing on the closed drop-down starts a search (#304).
    • Dashboard cards list the devices needing attention first (offline, then other trouble, then pending updates) and stop at ten, with a link to the rest. The Fleet strip stops at two rows of tiles. The update banner names six devices per line and counts the rest. Lists that can run long (things to handle, capacity when expanded, devices not polled recently) keep a fixed height and scroll (#303).
    • Topology asks for a site on a large fleet, and a device's page opens its own part of the map (Topology, #305).

    Is it keeping up?¶

    Settings → Polling, or GET /api/system/poller.

    The number to read is headroom — service capacity divided by demand:

    arrival   sum over devices of 1 / that device's fast-poll interval
service   worker concurrency / mean poll duration
headroom  service / arrival

    Below 1.0 the backlog grows every cycle and devices go stale. Comfortably above it, the fleet is being polled on schedule.

    Headroom alone is not enough, which is why backlog and drain estimate sit beside it. It describes flow — whether the workers can keep up from here — and is blind to work already queued. A healthy headroom next to a large backlog means capacity is fine but you are still working through a queue.

    A single status summarises all of it: ok, draining, or saturated.

    Which devices are being missed¶

    The same page lists devices not polled recently, and distinguishes two states that look identical from the outside:

    • not attempted recently — the poller never reached it
    • responded with… — it was reached and did not answer

    device_poll_stats records attempt and success separately per device and per kind, with duration and last error. Their difference is that distinction.

    Tuning¶

    All of these are environment variables and need a container restart.

    Variable Default Effect
    POLLER_CONCURRENCY 12 Workers per queue. The first lever when headroom is low
    POLLER_INTERVAL_MS 10000 Scheduler tick: how often it checks what's due. Polls run at their own intervals; this only needs to be as short as the shortest of them (10 s is the shortest fast poll)
    POLLER_JOB_TIMEOUT_MS 45000 Ceiling on one poll, so a dead device cannot hold a worker. A poll that runs past it is cancelled and its connection closed
    POLLER_JOB_RETENTION_SEC 3600 How long finished jobs are kept
    POLLER_JOB_RETENTION_COUNT 5000 How many finished jobs are kept
    REDIS_MAXMEMORY 512mb Ceiling on Redis

    Raise concurrency first. Lengthen the poll intervals second (in Settings, or for the sites that need it) — it reduces freshness, which is the thing you are paying for.

    When a poll hits the time limit. A fast poll that runs out of time counts as a failed poll (the device goes offline if it keeps happening). A slow poll that runs out of time is simply cancelled; it says nothing about whether the device is up, so it never marks the device offline. Any open outage is closed by the next successful poll. Before 0.24.37 a poll that hit the limit kept running after it had been counted as failed, and when it finished it marked the device online without closing the outage it had opened, so the device's uptime kept falling while it was up.

    More workers¶

    One backend runs POLLER_CONCURRENCY polls at a time. When headroom stays low after raising that, run extra poll workers next to it (#301):

    docker compose --profile workers up -d --scale poller=3

    Each poller container runs POLLER_CONCURRENCY polls at once against the same Redis and Postgres, and nothing else: no API, no schedule, no firmware rollouts, reports or backups, which stay with the backend. Their live updates reach the browser through the backend. Settings → Polling counts every process's workers (Workers: 36, in 3 processes), and headroom uses the total. To leave all polling to the workers, set POLLER_ROLE=api on the backend in .env.

    POLLER_ROLE Runs
    all (default) The API, the schedule and poll workers
    api The API and the schedule; polls go to poller containers
    worker Poll workers only (what the poller service sets)

    Worth knowing:

    • With sessions kept open, they're kept per process, and a device's polls go to whichever worker is free, so with several workers fewer polls find their session open, and a device can hold up to three sessions per worker. Plenty of workers is still the faster way through a large fleet; a device that can take only a few API sessions is the case to watch.
    • Workers read the backend's secrets from the app_data volume (read-only), and start once the backend is healthy, so its migrations have run.
    • The pre-built docker-compose.ghcr.yml has the same poller service.

    Reducing the work itself¶

    Raising concurrency changes how much can be done at once. Switching off a collector changes how much there is to do, which is the larger lever on a big fleet: every collector is a round trip per device per cycle.

    Settings → Polling → Collectors.

    Collector Cadence Cost of switching it off
    Connected clients fast poll No client list, per-client traffic or fingerprinting
    Neighbour discovery slow poll Topology stops updating; no automatic discovery
    Device logs own 60s poll No events; log alerts go quiet. The session is skipped entirely
    Certificates slow poll No certificate inventory or expiry warnings

    Measured on the reference fleet, turning off clients and logs:

    logs   694 ms  ->    1 ms     (returns before connecting)
fast   562 ms  ->  211 ms     (-62%)

    Each one costs the feature it feeds, and data already collected stops being refreshed rather than disappearing — pages will show figures that quietly age. That is the trade.

    Event volume¶

    Events are device log lines, and they are the largest table by a wide margin on most installs.

    retention_events_days (default 30) is enforced hourly, deleting in batches so that a first run on a long-unpruned install does not hold locks for the duration.

    Until v0.24.19 that setting existed and was read by nothing, so events grew without limit. If you are upgrading from an older build, expect a large one-off delete on the first poll cycle after the upgrade — 109,649 rows on a five-device fleet that had been running for 36 days.

    Behaviour worth knowing¶

    A device already queued is not enqueued again. Deduplication is by job id, so a backlog cannot compound: one outstanding poll per device and kind, however long it waits.

    Periodic polls are not retried in-job. The schedule is the retry — a device that did not answer is unlikely to answer a second later, and a retry occupies a worker the rest of the fleet needs.

    Stale queued polls are discarded, at startup and every five minutes. A periodic poll that has waited longer than its own cadence is worthless: running it produces a reading the next cycle would take anyway. Discarding is safe precisely because the work is periodic — the schedule re-enqueues within one interval.

    Job history is bounded. Redis runs with noeviction rather than an LRU policy on purpose: BullMQ's job locks and stalled-check keys carry TTLs, and evicting one causes duplicate processing or a stalled-job storm. A memory ceiling protects the host; retention keeps usage far below it.

    Clearing a backlog¶

    Settings → Polling → Clear queued, or POST /api/system/poller/drain.

    Nothing is lost. The scheduler re-enqueues whatever is still due on its next tick, so the cost is at most one interval of freshness.

    Scheduled work and timezones¶

    Scheduled tasks are evaluated in the timezone set under Settings → General, not the container's. The container runs UTC, so without this a backup set for 02:00 would fire at 02:00 UTC wherever you are — and on the wrong day far enough east.
